Start

What ungoggled does

ungoggled turns a Raspberry Pi 4B into a receiver for your DJI FPV goggles: the picture from the goggles appears on any HDMI screen, so friends can watch along on a TV or monitor.

The goggles send their live picture over a USB cable to the Pi. The Pi shows it on the screen connected to its HDMI port, can record it, and can stream it to spectators' phones. You control everything from the web console: a web page served by the Pi itself, opened on your phone over the Pi's own Wi-Fi. No internet and no app are needed.

Tip

Every panel in the console has a small ? in its top-right corner. It opens the matching chapter of this help.

Connect & first start

Four connections, then open the console on your phone.

Which cable goes where

  1. Goggles → Pi USB-C port. Use a USB-C data cable from the goggles to the Pi's USB-C port (the one normally used for power). The original DJI FPV Goggles V1/V2 are the exception: they go into one of the Pi's USB-A ports.
  2. Screen → HDMI0. Connect the screen with a micro-HDMI cable to HDMI0, the micro-HDMI port right next to the USB-C port.
  3. Power the Pi separately, through its 5 V GPIO pins (for example with a power hat) or PoE. The goggles' cable does not power it.
  4. Switch everything on. Check that the goggles show a live camera picture.
Clean picture

For video without the goggles' on-screen display (battery, channel, signal bars), set in the goggles: Settings → Camera → Advanced Camera Settings → Camera View Recording → OFF. Switch it on if you want those overlays on the screen and in recordings. The Pi cannot remove overlays that are already in the picture.

Open the console

  1. On your phone, join the Wi-Fi ungoggled with the password ungoggled (the defaults of a fresh SD card; change them later under Wi-Fi).
  2. Open http://192.168.50.1 in the browser. ungoggled.local also works on most devices.
  3. The very first visit asks you to choose a password (at least 8 characters).
Expected warnings

Your phone may say the Wi-Fi has no internet. That is normal: stay connected. Over https:// the browser warns once that the connection isn't private, because the Pi makes its own certificate. Tap Advanced, then Proceed.

Install the console as an app

Put the console on your phone's home screen so it opens full screen, like an app.

In Chrome, open the menu and choose Install app (or Add to Home Screen). That gives a real app icon with its own entry in the phone's app list.

Chrome only offers that for a page it fully trusts. The Pi uses a self-signed certificate (there is no public domain name for the Pi's own Wi-Fi to get a normal one), so most of the time Chrome offers only Create shortcut. That still opens full screen without the address bar and is fine for everyday use.

Want the real app entry? Trust the certificate once per phone

  1. Download the certificate: /api/tls-cert.crt
  2. Android: Settings → Security (or “Security & privacy”) → Encryption & credentials → Install a certificate → CA certificate, then pick the downloaded file.
  3. Open the console again. Chrome should now offer Install app.

This is entirely optional. The certificate isn't secret: it is exactly what your browser already receives every time it connects to the Pi.

Using it

Preview & receiver

Where: Live tab · System tab

The preview box on the Live tab is a small copy of what goes out over HDMI, so you can check the picture without looking at the screen.

It is a small, low-frame-rate picture (up to 640 × 360 at 5 pictures per second), not the HDMI picture itself. Switching the preview off saves a little work and Wi-Fi traffic, but never touches the HDMI output.

The status boxes

Goggles input shows the picture size, frames per second and bitrate arriving from the goggles, and what the connection is doing: Waiting for goggles, Connecting goggles, Goggles connected, Waiting for camera (goggles there, no video yet), Receiving video. HDMI output shows the screen's mode, refresh rate and the rendered frames per second.

A 60 Hz HDMI signal may carry a 30 fps camera picture. The console shows both numbers separately: the refresh rate is the HDMI signal timing, the rendered fps is how many camera pictures reach the screen. A higher HDMI rate does not make the camera faster.

Reconnect goggles

On the Live tab. Starts a fresh connection to the goggles without restarting everything. Try this first if the picture gets stuck.

Start / Stop receiver

On the System tab. Stops listening to the goggles entirely; the screen keeps showing the idle screen. Mostly useful for troubleshooting: normally leave it running. The receiver starts by itself when the Pi starts and recovers on its own after the cable was pulled.

How Automatic finds your goggles

  1. Goggles plugged in. The Pi recognises the pair of goggles.
  2. Have these goggles shown a picture on this Pi before?
    Yes, rememberedThe Pi uses only the way that worked last time. It never tries the other generation on them again.
    No, new gogglesThe Pi tries the Goggles 3 way first. Without a picture after a few seconds it tries the Goggles 2 way, then goes back, waiting longer each round (up to two minutes).
  3. First picture arrives. The way that worked is remembered for this pair of goggles.

“Remembered” means: once a pair of goggles has shown a picture, the Pi knows which start sequence they need and sticks to it. That matters because Goggles 3 whose drone is simply not switched on yet must not be sent the Goggles 2 start sequence.

When to pick a fixed type

  • Your goggles are not found with Automatic.
  • You have FPV Goggles V1/V2 (Automatic never looks for them).
  • You want a setup that already works to stay exactly as it is: a fixed choice never tries anything else.

Changing the type restarts the receiver; the goggles connect again by themselves within a few seconds.

For helpers

If UNGOGGLED_GOGGLES is set in /etc/default/dji-hdmi on the Pi, that value is used while the console says Automatic; the panel then shows System default: …. What happened is written to the log: search for Goggles protocol:.

Output mode

Automatic uses 1080p at up to 60 Hz, which is right for almost everyone. You can also pick any other mode the screen offers, including interlaced modes like 1080i for equipment that needs them. Interlaced modes are listed by field rate, so 1080i60 carries 30 full pictures per second. The picture is scaled to the chosen resolution, and applying a mode interrupts the output briefly.

4K at 60 Hz

If the screen can do 4K (3840 × 2160) at 50 or 60 Hz, those modes are listed too. Automatic still uses 1080p.

4K60 needs two things
  • The HDMI0 port, next to the USB-C port. The other port can't do it.
  • A high-speed (HDMI 2.0) cable.

Black picture after choosing a 4K mode? Choose Automatic again and press Apply. If the picture does not come back, restart the Pi (System tab → Reboot Pi).

Stretch to fill the screen

Normally a picture is shown in its own shape, so a 4:3 picture (many action cameras, or the goggles in 4:3 mode) gets black bars at the sides of a 16:9 screen. The two stretch switches pull it over the whole screen instead; round things then look slightly oval. Both are off by default and the speed is the same either way. Switching the live one restarts the live picture once.

The Pi's display chip cannot stretch every live picture size. Such a picture stays unstretched, after a brief flicker when it starts.

Idle screen

What the screen shows while there's no live picture: before the goggles connect, or when the signal drops. Built in are Dark screen, three looping videos (Logo loop, Logo loop 3D, FPV comic) and a “No signal” picture. The built-in ones can't be deleted.

The button next to each entry says what tapping it does: Play appears while the idle screen is on the TV (it switches right away); Use appears while live video is showing (it becomes the next idle screen without interrupting the picture). The choice survives restarts.

Your own picture or video

Tap Upload image or video. An upload becomes the idle screen straight away.

Recording experimental

Where: buttons on Live · switches in Settings › Recording · files in Videos

Saves the live goggles picture to the SD card exactly as it arrives, with no quality loss.

If the goggles' on-screen display (battery, channel, signal bars) is switched on in the goggles, it is part of the picture and of the recording. That is set on the goggles or in DJI Fly, not here.

Recording?
1Start recording
2Save last 30 seconds now
Settings › Recording
3Auto-record every flight
4Crash buffer (last 30 seconds)
5Record to USB drive instead of SD card
  1. 1Start / Stop recording: you decide when it starts and stops.
  2. 2Save last 30 seconds now: grabs roughly the last half-minute, any time.
  3. 3Auto-record every flight: nothing to press; keeps the 5 newest flights.
  4. 4Crash buffer: when the signal drops, the last 30 seconds are saved by themselves; keeps the 3 newest clips.
  5. 5Record to USB drive: saves to a USB stick instead of the SD card.

Which one should I use?

Auto-record every flight

Switch it on and forget it. Every time you fly, a recording starts by itself and is saved the moment the signal ends. The 5 most recent flights are kept; the oldest is replaced automatically.

Crash buffer (last 30 seconds)

Like the instant replay in the DJI goggles. When the signal drops (say, in a crash) the last 30 seconds are saved on their own. Keeps the 3 most recent clips, separately from the 5 flights.

Start recording

Manual control: start and stop yourself. Writes straight to the card while it records. Works alongside the switches above; all can run at once.

Save last 30 seconds now

For a moment you didn't plan to record and the signal never dropped. Works whether the crash buffer is on or off.

Long flights

With auto-record, a long flight is saved in parts (flight-…-2.h264 and so on) when the Pi's memory fills up. All parts of one flight count as one of the 5 kept flights. Switching the Pi off waits until a copy has finished.

Where do recordings go?

By default to the SD card. Switch on Record to USB drive instead of SD card and plug a USB stick or drive into a USB-A port to save there instead: handy for long sessions, or to take the footage home on the stick. Without a drive plugged in, recordings stay on the SD card.

Card full

With less than 2 GB free on the SD card, recording stops on its own and the console shows Storage is full; recording stopped. After the goggles' picture ends, the screen shows an “SD card full” notice (it never interrupts a live picture for that). Delete some recordings under Videos.

Download, play, delete

The Recordings list on the Videos tab shows every file with a thumbnail. You can download it (big files stream straight from the card, and a broken download can continue), play it on the screen with Play on HDMI, or delete it (the console asks first).

USB – ActionCam video player experimental

Where: Videos tab

Plug an action camera or a USB stick into the Pi and play its videos on the screen. Handy for watching footage together without a laptop.

  1. Plug the camera (as USB storage) or stick into one of the Pi's USB-A ports, not the USB-C port the goggles use.
  2. Its videos appear in the list, each with a thumbnail (.mp4, .mov, .mkv, .avi, .m4v, .insv, .lrv).
  3. Tap a video to play it on the screen. Play/pause and seeking are done in the console; only the picture goes to the screen.

The Pi only opens drives formatted as FAT/FAT32, exFAT or NTFS (what cameras, memory cards and Windows use). A drive formatted differently (ext4, for example) is never opened. A drive that can't be opened is tried again every 30 seconds. The drive is only read, never changed.

How smoothly is it playing?

While a clip plays, a line under the time bar shows how many pictures per second really reach the screen and how fast the clip runs compared with real time (1.00× is real time). Very large action-camera clips can be more than the Pi can decode in real time; they then play in smooth slow motion, and this line tells you how slow. The same number appears in the HDMI output box and as the “TV” line of the frame-rate graph.

Pulling out the camera while a clip plays stops the player and brings the idle screen back.

Does it work with my camera?

Most common action cameras and formats work, but not every model has been tested. If a video doesn't show up or doesn't play cleanly, that's a known risk of this experimental feature.

Spectator stream experimental

Where: Live tab › Spectator stream

People standing around watch the live picture on their own phone, in a plain browser, over the Pi's Wi-Fi.

This is completely separate from the link you fly with; switching it on or off never affects your flight or the HDMI screen. Spectators see the picture with a delay of a second or two. It's off by default.

High or low quality?

High quality

The original goggles picture, close to what the HDMI screen shows. Realistic for one or two viewers at once.

Low quality

Smaller and a bit softer, but noticeably more viewers can watch at the same time.

Only one of the two runs at a time; switching one on turns the other off. The Pi's Wi-Fi radio is the real limit, and it is shared by all viewers and the console itself.

How do spectators watch?

  1. Tap Show QR codes for spectators. The screen shows two codes.
  2. The first code joins the Pi's Wi-Fi (it contains the current network name and password).
  3. The second opens the stream page /watch.html. Nothing to install.

Their phone may say the Wi-Fi has no internet; that's expected. The first time, the browser warns that the connection isn't private: Advanced, then Proceed.

The stream page never asks for the console password, so you can share it with anyone without giving them control.

What the beeper and LEDs tell you

  • Goggles, video and HDMI each beep when they connect (a happy tone) and when they disconnect (a sad tone). The three events sound different, so you can tell them apart by ear.
  • The receiver plays one chime at startup.
  • Each LED lights up while its state is true and goes off otherwise: goggles attached, video live, HDMI screen connected.
  • Beep every 5 seconds until the clock is synced (off by default) keeps chirping after the Pi starts until a browser has opened the console (or the Pi got the time from the internet). Then your recordings get the right date and time.

Raspberry Pi 4B GPIO pin diagram

The 40 pins on the edge of the Pi, which one is which, and which ones ungoggled uses.

GPIO number is not the pin number

Every pin has two numbers, and they are almost never the same:

  • The physical pin number (1 to 40) is simply its position on the header, counted from pin 1.
  • The GPIO number (also called BCM number) is the name the Pi's chip gives it. The console always wants the GPIO number.
A typical mix-up

A button is wired to physical pin 5 and 5 is typed into the console. But physical pin 5 is GPIO 3, and “5” in the console means GPIO 5, which sits on physical pin 29, at the other end of the header. The button does nothing.

typed5→GPIO 5 = pin 29

Wrong for a button on pin 5.

typed3→GPIO 3 = pin 5

Right: the button on physical pin 5.

Finding pin 1

Hold the Pi with the pin header along the top edge: the USB-C port and the two micro-HDMI ports are then at the bottom left, the USB-A ports and the network socket on the right, the microSD card at the left edge (underneath). Pin 1 is at the left end of the header, in the row closer to the middle of the board; on the back of the board it has a square solder pad (all others are round). Pin 2 is right next to it, at the board's edge.

All 40 pins

Drawn the usual way: header standing upright, pin 1 at the top left. Odd pins are in the left column, even pins in the right column. Tap a colour to show only those pins.

Careful
  • The Pi's GPIO pins work with 3.3 V. Never connect 5 V to a GPIO pin.
  • Never connect a 5 V or 3.3 V pin directly to GND: that is a short circuit.
  • Wire with the Pi switched off.

GPIO 0 and 1 (pins 27 and 28) are reserved for identifying hats; the console only accepts GPIO 2 to 27. GPIO 2 and 3 (pins 3 and 5) are also the pins hats use to talk to the Pi (I²C, marked SDA/SCL).

Wiring examples

Every part connects between one GPIO pin and one GND pin. Any GND pin works; the nearest one keeps the wires short.

A status LED (example: the goggles LED)

Use plain (non-addressable) LEDs with one series resistor each, for example about 330 Ω. The video LED (GPIO 23, pin 16) and HDMI LED (GPIO 24, pin 18) are wired exactly the same way; pin 14 and pin 20 are GND pins close to them.

The piezo beeper

A push button (shutdown button or extra button)

Buttons need no resistor: the pin is held “high” inside the Pi, and pressing the button connects it to GND. Use a momentary switch (springs back when released), not a latching on/off switch.

The default shutdown button (GPIO 19, pin 35) is wired the same way, with GND on pin 34 or 39. GPIO 19 is the default because it matches the NuclearHazard V5 hat. For a button that can also start the Pi, see Power button.

Extra buttons new

Where: Settings › Beeper, LEDs & buttons › Extra buttons

Up to four more push buttons, each with one job. Each one is a momentary switch between a GPIO pin and a GND pin (wiring).

Adding a button

  1. Wire the button between a free GPIO pin and a GND pin (see the pin diagram for free pins).
  2. Tap Add a button. A new row appears.
  3. Type the GPIO number (not the pin number), pick the action and the hold time.
  4. Tap Apply.

The hold time can be changed for every button: Tap, or hold for 1, 2, 3 or 5 seconds. Shut down and reboot start with 2 seconds so that a knock in your bag doesn't switch the Pi off.

Example: a “clip” button on the case

Push button between pin 37 (GPIO 26) and pin 39 (GND). In the console: Add a button, GPIO 26, Save the last 30 seconds, Tap, Apply. Press it after a great line and the last half-minute is saved.

Running your own script

A button with Run a script runs a file of your own. The console only lets you type the file's name; you have to put the file onto the Pi yourself, over SSH or the terminal, into the folder /var/lib/dji-hdmi/button-scripts/, and make it executable. It runs as the Pi's administrator (root) and is stopped after 60 seconds.

Example: a button that restarts the Pi's Wi-Fi (for someone who knows the command line):

mkdir -p /var/lib/dji-hdmi/button-scripts
printf '#!/bin/sh\nsystemctl restart NetworkManager\n' > /var/lib/dji-hdmi/button-scripts/wifi-restart.sh
chmod +x /var/lib/dji-hdmi/button-scripts/wifi-restart.sh

Then type wifi-restart.sh as the script name. Names may contain only letters, digits, -, _ and ..

Every press is written to the log, together with the reason when nothing happened (an update being installed, no clip to save yet, a script that failed).

Power button: one switch to shut down and start

A button between physical pin 5 (GPIO 3) and physical pin 6 (GND, right next to it) can both shut the Pi down and start it again.

Setting it up

  1. Wire a momentary push button between physical pin 5 and physical pin 6.
  2. In Settings › Beeper, LEDs & buttons tap Add power button (GPIO 3). This adds GPIO 3 → Shut down the Pi with a 2-second hold.
  3. Tap Apply.

How it works

  • Pi running: hold the button for 2 seconds and the Pi shuts down properly.
  • Pi shut down: a short press starts it again. This is done by the Pi's own start-up firmware, not by ungoggled: nothing runs while the Pi is off, and nothing else has to be set up.
  • Power simply cut (battery pulled): the button does not start it. The Pi starts by itself as soon as power returns.
For helpers

Starting from GPIO 3 needs the Pi 4 bootloader settings WAKE_ON_GPIO=1 (the default) and POWER_OFF_ON_HALT=0. Check them with vcgencmd bootloader_config.

Fan

An optional fan on GPIO 14 (physical pin 8) switches on by itself from 60 °C.

This is set in the Pi's start-up configuration, not in the console, so there is nothing to switch on there. It became active with the update that added it, after one restart of the Pi.

A GPIO pin can only give the on/off signal; it cannot power a fan by itself. Use a fan or fan board that takes a control signal, or a small switching (transistor) board in between, and follow its instructions.

Is the Pi getting hot? The temperature is on the System tab and in the graphs; 75 °C or more is shown as a warning.

Network & access

Wi-Fi

Where: Settings › Wi-Fi access point

The Pi makes its own Wi-Fi network, so you can reach the console and spectators can watch. It doesn't need your home or field Wi-Fi at all.

Changing the network name and password

Enter a new SSID (network name, up to 32 characters) and a New password (8 to 63 characters), then tap Apply Wi-Fi. The change applies right away, so your phone loses the connection: join the network again with the new name and password. The HDMI picture keeps running meanwhile.

A fresh SD card uses ungoggled / ungoggled, which everyone can look up. Change it before you take the Pi to a busy field. The Pi's own network uses 5 GHz.

Joining an existing Wi-Fi network instead

Useful for a Pi that stays put as a review station at home or at the club.

  1. Under Join an existing Wi-Fi network, tap Scan for networks and pick one, or type its name.
  2. Enter its password (leave it empty for an open network) and join.
  3. The Pi switches off its own network and joins that one. Connect your phone to that same network too.
  4. Find the Pi at ungoggled.local, or read its address off the HDMI screen.
Safe to try

If joining doesn't succeed within 30 seconds, the Pi gives up and brings its own network back by itself. No restart needed.

After a restart

Always broadcast the access point after a reboot decides what happens the next time the Pi starts. On (default): it always comes back with its own network, which is what you want for flying. Off: it tries to rejoin the network it was last connected to, good for a stationary Pi.

Password

Where: Settings › Password (at the bottom)

One password for everything: the console, the Pi's SSH/root login and the terminal.

Anyone with this password can control the receiver, change settings and reach your recordings. The first visit to the console asks you to set one, at least 8 characters long; after that your browser stays logged in. Until you set it, the SSH/root login and the terminal use ungoggled, so set yours before you take the Pi anywhere.

After an update or a restart of the Pi, everyone has to log in again.

The spectator page (/watch.html) is the one exception on purpose: it never asks for the password, so you can share that link without handing over control.

Changing it

In the Password panel enter the current password once and the new one twice, then Change password. The SSH/root password changes with it, and all your other phones and computers have to log in again.

Too many wrong tries

After 5 wrong passwords in a row, that device is locked out for 5 minutes. Many wrong passwords from different devices slow the login down for everybody.

Forgot the password? Reset it with the SD card

Because SSH uses the same password, there's no way back in over the network. Use the SD card instead:

  1. Turn the Pi off and take out the SD card.
  2. Put the card into a computer. Windows or macOS shows a small drive with files like config.txt on it (the other part of the card can't be opened there, and doesn't need to be).
  3. Create an empty file named exactly reset-password on that small drive: no .txt or any other ending. Windows hides file endings by default, so check the name really has none.
  4. Eject the card, put it back into the Pi and switch it on.
  5. Done. While starting, the Pi removes the console password, sets SSH/root back to ungoggled and deletes the file. The next visit to the console asks for a new password, like the very first time.

If the console refuses every login with a message pointing to this reset, the password file on the Pi is damaged. The same reset fixes it.

System & upkeep

System & clock

Where: System tab › System

The Pi's clock, restarting and shutting down safely, and the diagnostics download.

The clock

The Pi has no battery-backed clock, so after a power cut it doesn't know the time. Opening the console sets it from your phone automatically. Sync Time does the same by hand. The time matters for the date and time of your recordings and of the log.

Reboot and shut down

Reboot Pi and Shut down Pi both ask before they do anything. Shut the Pi down before pulling the power when you can; a power button can do it without the phone.

“Restart the Pi once to finish the last update”

Some updates also improve how the Pi itself starts up, for example keeping the video chip fast enough for large action-cam clips. Those parts only switch on the next time the Pi starts. Until then everything works exactly as before, so there's no rush: restart it when it suits you (never mid-flight) with Reboot Pi, or it simply happens the next time you power the Pi up. The message goes away once the Pi is back up.

Download diagnostics

Having a problem? Download diagnostics gathers the log and system information into one file you can download, even on your phone, and send along when asking for help. Nothing is uploaded anywhere on its own. What's inside: see Log & diagnostics.

Graphs (last 30 minutes)

Small graphs of the last half hour, so you can see what happened during the last flight.

Terminal

Where: switch on in Settings › Terminal · open in System › Terminal

A command line on the Pi, right in the browser: for troubleshooting together with someone who knows Linux, without SSH.

It runs as the Pi's administrator (root), so a wrong command can break the installation. If you don't know what a command does, don't run it.

Switching it on

It's off until you switch it on in Settings › Terminal. Switching it off again closes an open terminal immediately.

Safety limits

  • Every time you open it, it asks for the password again, even though you're logged in. Wrong guesses count towards the same lock-out as the login page.
  • Only one terminal can be open at a time.
  • It closes after 10 minutes without typing, after 30 minutes at most, and when you log out; on most phones also a minute after you switch away from it. Anything still running in it is stopped.

On a phone

The row of keys under the terminal adds what a phone keyboard lacks: Esc, Tab, Ctrl (tap it, then the letter), the arrow keys and a few symbols; A− and A+ change the text size. The buttons above run a few harmless look-up commands, like the service log or free disk space.

Software updates

Where: System tab › Software update and Installed versions

Updates come through your phone. If a new version doesn't start properly, the Pi goes back to the old one by itself.

Checking for a new version

The line at the top says whether your phone can reach the update server right now: green means it can; amber means it can't (often because the phone is on the Pi's Wi-Fi without mobile data). It's checked automatically when you open the System tab; Check for updates checks again and Release notes shows what's new before you download anything.

The download uses your phone's own mobile data, not the Pi, so it works out in the field. Once downloaded, install it right away or keep it ready to install later.

Installing from a file

No connection? Get the release's .update.tar.gz file (smaller than 64 MiB) onto your phone or computer, choose it in the Software update panel or drag and drop it there. The video pauses during installation; settings that still fit the new version are kept.

What happens during an update

  1. File checked. The Pi makes sure the file is a complete, valid update.
  2. Settings backed up, new version installed. The picture pauses for a moment.
  3. Health check. The new version has to prove itself on the HDMI screen.
    PassesThe new version is kept.
    FailsThe previous version comes back automatically, with its settings. Nothing stays broken.

An update rolled back and offers to skip the health check

Occasionally the check itself is wrong although the new version looked fine on the HDMI screen; this has happened before. When a rollback happens for exactly that reason, a Retry without the health check button appears. It installs the update again without that automatic check. It is only offered in that situation, never for a broken download or a real problem.

Installed versions: going back

The SD card keeps the active version, the one before it, one older update and the version from the SD-card image. Switch version uses the same safe procedure as an update: settings are backed up and the previous state returns automatically if the version does not start.

Some updates need one restart to finish; see Restart the Pi once.

Log & diagnostics new

Where: System tab › Download diagnostics

The Pi writes down what happens, so a problem can be understood after the flight, even after the battery was pulled.

How long it is kept

The log is kept on the SD card for 30 days, also across restarts and power cuts. It never grows past 100 MB: the oldest part is dropped first, so it can't fill the card.

What's in the diagnostics download

  • the receiver's log (everything that is kept),
  • the Pi's own messages: USB events, under-voltage, SD-card errors,
  • the list of starts (boots) of the Pi,
  • which way each pair of goggles was remembered with,
  • the USB state.
Example

The picture froze during the third flight. At home, open the console, tap Download diagnostics and send the file to whoever helps you. It still shows when the goggles connected, which way they used, and when the picture stopped.

What to look for

Search forTells you
Goggles protocol:which way the goggles were started, and how many seconds after the start the first picture came
status phase:when the picture started and stopped
usb_state:the goggles' USB connection
power/thermal:under-voltage (weak power supply) or overheating
heartbeat:a one-line summary every minute while goggles are attached
DUMLthe first message of each kind the goggles send
Times

The times in the log are only right once the clock was set (clock synced: true), because the Pi has no battery clock. Opening the console sets it.

On the Pi itself (for helpers)

Over SSH or in the terminal:

journalctl -u dji-hdmi              # everything kept, oldest first
journalctl -u dji-hdmi -b -1        # the start before this one
journalctl _TRANSPORT=kernel        # the Pi's own messages

Help

Troubleshooting

Symptom → cause → fix. Tap a question to open it.

No picture on the screen
Check first
On the Live tab, look at the Goggles input box.
“Nothing powering the accessory port”
The Pi doesn't see the goggles on USB-C. Check that the cable goes into the Pi's USB-C port, that it is a data cable, and that the goggles are on. FPV Goggles V1/V2 belong in a USB-A port, with the goggles type set to V1/V2.
“Waiting for camera”
The goggles are connected but no video arrives yet. Make sure the drone/air unit is on and the goggles themselves show a picture.
Still nothing
Tap Reconnect goggles. Then see Goggles not found.
The HDMI output box says no display
Check the micro-HDMI cable and that the screen is on and set to the right input. Use HDMI0, next to the USB-C port.
Goggles are not found
Cause
Automatic tries the Goggles 3 way first and switches only after a few seconds without a picture. FPV Goggles V1/V2 are never found by Automatic.
Fix
Pick your goggles' type in Settings › Goggles (details). The receiver restarts and the goggles connect again within a few seconds. If it still doesn't work, download diagnostics and look for Goggles protocol:.
Black screen after choosing a 4K mode
Cause
4K at 60 Hz works only on HDMI0 (next to the USB-C port) with a high-speed HDMI 2.0 cable.
Fix
Choose Automatic under Settings › HDMI and tap Apply. If the picture does not come back, restart the Pi (System › Reboot Pi).
Black bars at the sides of the picture
Cause
The picture is 4:3 and the screen 16:9; it is shown in its own shape.
Fix
Switch on Stretch the live picture to fill the screen (or the USB one) under Settings › HDMI.
I can't log in
Forgot the password
Reset it with an empty file named reset-password on the SD card: step by step.
“Locked”
After 5 wrong passwords in a row the device is locked out for 5 minutes. Wait, then try again.
Every login is refused with a hint about the reset
The password file is damaged; the same reset fixes it.
Logged out suddenly
Normal after an update, a restart of the Pi, or a password change on another device.
The console doesn't open or says “Disconnected”
Check
Is your phone on the Pi's Wi-Fi? Some phones leave a Wi-Fi without internet; tell the phone to stay connected. Then open http://192.168.50.1.
The Pi joined another network
Its own network is off then. Connect your phone to that network and open ungoggled.local, or read the Pi's address off the HDMI screen. If joining failed, the Pi's own network comes back after about 30 seconds.
The picture stutters or a USB video plays slowly
USB videos
Very large action-camera clips are more than the Pi can decode in real time and play in smooth slow motion. The speed line under the time bar shows how slow (1.00× is real time).
Live picture
Look at the graphs: camera vs. TV frame rate, temperature (warning from 75 °C). In the log, power/thermal: lines mean under-voltage (power supply too weak) or overheating.
Good to know
A 60 Hz HDMI signal with a 30 fps camera picture is normal, not a fault. The spectator stream never affects the HDMI picture or your flight.
My USB drive or camera isn't shown
Port
Use a USB-A port, not the USB-C port of the goggles.
Format
Only FAT/FAT32, exFAT and NTFS drives are opened. A drive formatted as ext4, for example, is ignored.
Files
Only video files are listed (.mp4, .mov, .mkv, .avi, .m4v, .insv, .lrv). A drive that couldn't be opened is retried every 30 seconds.
The Pi doesn't start with the button
Right pins?
Only physical pin 5 (GPIO 3) with pin 6 (GND) can start the Pi. Another GPIO can shut it down, but not start it. See the pin-number mix-up.
Shut down or power cut?
The button starts a Pi that was shut down properly. After the power was simply cut, the Pi starts by itself when power returns.
For helpers
vcgencmd bootloader_config must show WAKE_ON_GPIO=1 and POWER_OFF_ON_HALT=0.
A button does nothing
GPIO vs. pin number
The console wants the GPIO number. Look the pin up in the pin table.
Hold time
Shut down and reboot start with a 2-second hold. Keep the button pressed long enough, or set a shorter hold.
Wiring
The button must connect the GPIO pin to a GND pin, and be a momentary switch.
Still nothing
Every press is in the log, with the reason when nothing happened (for example: an update is being installed, no clip to save yet, a script failed).
The beeper stays silent
Fix
Check that Piezo beeper on GPIO is switched on with the right GPIO number (default 6 = pin 31) and applied. Then tap Test buzzer; it only works after Apply. Use a passive piezo between the GPIO pin and GND (wiring).
“Pin must be a GPIO number between 2 and 27” or “GPIO pins must all be different”
Cause
You typed a physical pin number (like 31 or 40), or two parts share a pin.
Fix
Type the GPIO number from the pin table, and give every part its own pin.
“Storage is full; recording stopped”
Cause
Less than 2 GB is free on the SD card.
Fix
Delete recordings under Videos, then dismiss the message. For long sessions, record to a USB drive.
Recordings or the log have the wrong date or time
Cause
The Pi has no battery clock and doesn't know the time after a power cut.
Fix
Open the console once after switching on: it sets the clock from your phone. Switch on Beep every 5 seconds until the clock is synced as a reminder.
An update went back to the old version
Cause
The new version didn't pass its health check on the HDMI screen, so the previous one came back. Nothing is broken.
Fix
If the new version actually looked fine, use Retry without the health check when it is offered (details). Otherwise send the diagnostics to whoever helps you.
“No internet” or “Your connection isn't private”
Both are expected
The Pi's Wi-Fi has no internet on purpose. The certificate warning appears because the Pi makes its own certificate: tap Advanced, then Proceed. To get rid of it for good on Android, see trusting the certificate.

Words used in this help

GPIO / BCM number
The name of a pin as the Pi's chip counts them (“GPIO 17”). This is what the console wants.
Physical pin number
The position on the 40-pin header, 1 to 40, counted from pin 1 (the square pad).
GND (ground)
The Pi's 0 V. Every button, LED and beeper has one wire to a GND pin. All GND pins are the same.
3.3 V / 5 V
Power pins. GPIO pins work with 3.3 V; never connect 5 V to a GPIO pin.
Momentary switch
A push button that springs back when released.
Pull-up
The Pi keeps a button pin “high” inside, so a press to GND is all it takes; no resistor needed.
Series resistor
The small resistor in line with an LED that limits its current (for example about 330 Ω).
Passive piezo
A thin disc that beeps when the Pi switches its pin on and off quickly.
Hold time
How long a button must be held before its action runs (tap, 1, 2, 3 or 5 s).
HDMI0
The micro-HDMI port next to the USB-C port; the only one that does 4K at 60 Hz.
Access point
The Pi's own Wi-Fi network.
SSID
The name of a Wi-Fi network.
Self-signed certificate
The Pi's own HTTPS certificate. Browsers warn about it once; it is safe to proceed on the Pi's Wi-Fi.
Idle screen
What the screen shows while there is no live picture.
Crash buffer
The last 30 seconds, kept in the background and saved when the signal drops.
Health check
After an update, the new version must show it works on the HDMI screen, or the old one comes back.
Rollback
Going back to the previous version automatically.
Diagnostics
A download with the log and system information, for asking for help.
