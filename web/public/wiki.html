Preview & receiver

What this does for you, in plain terms.

The big box on the main page is a small, low-frame-rate copy of what's going out over HDMI, so you can check everything looks right without a monitor plugged in. It is not the HDMI picture itself — turning it off saves a little bandwidth but never touches the actual output.

Start / Stop receiver

Stops listening to the goggles entirely. Mostly useful for troubleshooting; normally just leave it running.

Reconnect goggles

Forces a fresh handshake with the goggles without restarting the whole receiver — try this first if the picture gets stuck.

HDMI & idle screen

What this does for you, in plain terms.

Controls what the connected screen shows. "Output mode" picks the resolution; Automatic is right for almost everyone. The Pi has two micro-HDMI ports; either one works on its own. If you ever plug in two screens at once, "Output port" picks which one gets the picture.

Stretch to fill the screen

Normally a picture is shown in its own shape, so a 4:3 picture (many action cameras, or the goggles in 4:3 mode) has black bars at the sides of a 16:9 screen. Switch on "Stretch the live picture" and/or "Stretch USB videos" to pull it over the whole screen instead. Round things then look slightly oval. Both are off by default. The speed is the same either way. The Pi's display chip cannot stretch every picture size of the live feed; it then shows that picture as it arrives, after a brief flicker when the picture starts.

Idle screen

What the screen shows while there's no live picture — before the goggles connect, or if the signal drops. Pick one from the list: the built-in logo loops and "No signal" picture, a plain dark screen, or your own photo, logo or short looping video (up to 1920×1080, H.264, 64 MiB).

The button next to each one says what tapping it does. "Play" appears while the idle screen is what's on the TV: the TV switches to it right away. "Use" appears while live video is showing: it becomes the next idle screen without interrupting the picture.

The built-in ones are always there and can't be deleted. Room for your own: 3 videos and 16 pictures — the built-ins don't count towards that.

Recording

What this does for you, in plain terms.

Saves the live goggles picture to the SD card, exactly as it arrives — no quality loss. If the goggles' own on-screen display (battery, channel, signal bars) is turned on, it's part of the picture; that's set on the goggles or in DJI Fly, not here.

Auto-record every flight

Switch it on and forget it. Every time you fly, a recording starts by itself and is saved the moment the signal ends — nothing to press. Keeps the 5 most recent flights; the oldest is replaced automatically.

Crash buffer (last 30 seconds)

Like the instant-replay on the DJI goggles themselves. This runs in the background all the time once switched on, and the moment the signal drops — say, in a crash — it automatically saves the last 30 seconds on its own. Keeps the 3 most recent clips. The "save last 30 seconds now" button below works independently, any time you want to grab a moment without losing the signal first.

Start recording / Save last 30 seconds now

Manual alternatives for when you want full control: start and stop a recording yourself, or grab whatever happened in roughly the last half-minute right now.

Where do recordings go?

By default, the SD card — downloadable, deletable or playable straight on the connected screen from this page. If the card gets too full, recording stops on its own and you'll see a warning. Switch on "Record to USB drive instead of SD card" and plug a USB stick or drive into one of the regular USB-A ports to save recordings there instead — handy for longer sessions, or to pull footage straight off the drive afterwards. If the drive isn't plugged in, recordings fall back to the SD card automatically.

Spectator stream

What this does for you, in plain terms.

Lets people standing around watch the live feed on their own phone, over the Pi's own Wi-Fi — no extra app, just a browser. This is completely separate from the low-latency link you actually fly with; turning it on or off never affects your flight.

Which quality should I pick?

High quality — best picture, but realistically only for one or two people watching at once.

Low quality — a bit softer and smaller, but supports noticeably more viewers at the same time. Only one of the two runs at a time; switching one on turns the other off. The Pi's Wi-Fi radio is the real limit here, and it's shared with everyone watching plus the web interface itself.

How do spectators watch?

Two steps on their phone: first join the Pi's Wi-Fi, then open /watch.html in any browser. Nothing to install. "Show QR codes for spectators" puts both steps on your screen as two codes to scan, one after the other — one joins the Wi-Fi (it has the current network name and password in it), the other opens the stream page. Their phone may say the Wi-Fi has no internet; that's expected. The first time, their browser also warns that the connection isn't private, because the Pi makes its own certificate: tapping "Advanced", then "Proceed", gets past it.

USB – ActionCam video player

What this does for you, in plain terms.

Plug an action camera or a USB stick with videos into one of the Pi's regular USB-A ports (not the port the goggles use). Its video files show up in a list here, and you can play one straight on the connected screen — handy for watching footage together without a laptop.

How smoothly is it playing?

While a clip plays, a line under the time bar shows how many pictures per second really reach the screen and how fast the clip runs compared with real time (1.00× is real time). Very large action-camera clips can be more than the Pi can decode in real time; they then play in smooth slow motion, and this line tells you how slow. The same number appears in the HDMI output box and in the "TV" line of the frame-rate graph.

Does it work with my camera?

Most common action cameras and formats work, but it hasn't been tested against every model. If a video doesn't show up or doesn't play cleanly, that's a known risk of this still-experimental feature.

Wi-Fi access point

What this does for you, in plain terms.

The Pi broadcasts its own Wi-Fi network so you can reach this web interface, and so spectators can join the stream — it doesn't need your home or venue Wi-Fi at all. Change the name and password here; changes apply right away, so reconnect your own device afterwards too.

Joining an existing network instead

Further down, you can have the Pi join a Wi-Fi network you already have — scan for one nearby or type its name, enter the password and join. This switches off the Pi's own hotspot for as long as it's connected there, so reconnect your own device to that same network too; look for the Pi at ungoggled.local, or read its current address straight off the HDMI screen. If the join doesn't work out, the Pi gives up after about 30 seconds and goes back to broadcasting its own hotspot on its own — no reboot needed. The "always broadcast the access point after a reboot" switch decides what happens the next time the Pi is turned on: left on, it always comes back up as its own hotspot; switched off, it tries to rejoin the network it was last connected to instead — useful for a Pi that stays put as a stationary review station, rather than one that gets taken flying.

Beeper, LEDs & power button

What this does for you, in plain terms.

If you've wired up optional extras — a beeper, status LEDs or a physical power button — this is where you tell the Pi which pins they're connected to, or turn each one off if you don't have it.

System & clock

What this does for you, in plain terms.

The Pi has no battery-backed clock, so it doesn't know the real time after a power cut — opening this page sets it from your browser automatically. From here you can also reboot or shut the Pi down safely.

"Restart the Pi once to finish the last update"

Some updates also improve how the Pi itself starts up — for example keeping the video chip fast enough for large action-cam clips. Those parts only switch on the next time the Pi starts. Until then everything works exactly as before, so there's no rush: restart it when it suits you (never mid-flight) with "Reboot Pi" here, or it simply happens the next time you power the Pi up for your next session. The message goes away once the Pi is back up.

Download diagnostics

Having a problem? This button gathers recent logs, the update history and hardware info into one file you can download — even on your phone — and send along when asking for help. Nothing is uploaded anywhere on its own; it's just a download.

Graphs (last 30 minutes)

What this does for you, in plain terms.

Small graphs of the last half hour, grouped: Video (bitrate and frame rate), Pi health (temperature, processor and memory), Network (Wi-Fi and Ethernet traffic) and SD card. Touch or hover any graph to read the values at that moment from all of them at once; "Back to now" returns to the latest values. Shaded stretches had no live picture.

Values past a warning level turn amber and are spelled out at the top: the Pi at 75 °C or hotter, the processor or memory at 90 % or more, and the Wi-Fi sending 40 Mbps or more (close to what the Pi's radio can carry, which mostly matters with spectators watching).

Settings → Graphs switches whole groups on or off. That choice is remembered on each phone or computer separately. The history lives in the Pi's memory only, so it starts empty after a restart.

Terminal

What this does for you, in plain terms.

A command line on the Pi, right in the browser — for troubleshooting together with someone who knows Linux, without needing SSH. It runs as the Pi's administrator (root), so a wrong command can break the installation: if you don't know what a command does, don't run it.

Switching it on

It's off until you switch it on in Settings → Terminal. Switching it off again closes an open terminal immediately.

Safety limits

Every time you open it, it asks for the control password again, even though you're already logged in; wrong guesses count towards the same lock-out as the login page. Only one terminal can be open at a time. It closes on its own after 10 minutes without typing, after 30 minutes at most, and when you log out; on most phones also a minute after you switch away from it. Anything still running in it is stopped when it closes.

On a phone

The row of keys under the terminal adds what a phone keyboard lacks: Esc, Tab, Ctrl (tap it, then the letter), the arrow keys and a few symbols; A− and A+ change the text size. The buttons above them run a few harmless look-up commands, like the service log or free disk space.

Control password

What this does for you, in plain terms.

Everything on this page — settings, recordings, Wi-Fi, updates, rebooting the Pi — needs a password now, so anyone on the Wi-Fi can't change things just by finding this address. The first time anyone opens this page, it asks you to set one; after that, logging in keeps you signed in on that browser.

The spectator stream page (/watch.html) is the one exception on purpose — it never asks for this password, so you can hand that link to anyone without handing them control too.

Forgot the password? There's no reset button in the interface — ask whoever manages the Pi to delete auth.json from its data directory (/var/lib/dji-hdmi/ on the standard image) over SSH and restart the service; the next visit asks for a new password as if it were the first time.

Software updates

What this does for you, in plain terms.

The line at the top of this section says whether your phone can reach the update server right now: green means it can, amber means it can't (often because the phone is on the Pi's Wi-Fi without mobile data) — then upload an update file instead. It's checked automatically when you open the System tab.

Check for a new version right from this page, and see what's new before downloading anything. The download itself uses your phone's own mobile data, not the Pi's connection — so it works even out in the field. Once downloaded, install it right away or leave it ready to install later; either way, if something goes wrong on first start, it automatically switches back to the version that was working before, so you're never left with a broken device.

An update rolled back and offers to skip the health check

After installing, the new version has to prove itself on the HDMI screen before it's kept; if it doesn't, the previous version comes back automatically — nothing stays broken. Occasionally that check itself is wrong even though the new version actually looked fine, which has happened before. When a rollback happens for exactly that reason, a "Retry without the health check" button appears right there, letting you install it again without that automatic check. It's only offered for that specific situation — never for a corrupt download or a real problem.

Installing as an app

What this does for you, in plain terms.

In Chrome, tapping the menu and choosing "Install app" (or "Add to Home Screen") puts a real app icon on your phone that opens full screen, with its own entry in your phone's app list — not just a bookmark.

Chrome only offers that for a page it trusts as fully secure. This page uses a self-signed certificate (there's no public domain name for the Pi's own Wi-Fi to get a normal one from), so most of the time Chrome only offers "Create shortcut" instead — which still opens full screen without the address bar, so it already works fine for everyday use. If you'd rather have the real "app" entry, trust the certificate once per phone:

  1. Download the certificate: /api/tls-cert.crt
  2. Android: Settings → Security (or "Security & privacy") → Encryption & credentials → Install a certificate → CA certificate → pick the downloaded file
  3. Reopen this page — Chrome should now offer "Install app"

This is entirely optional. The certificate itself isn't secret — it's exactly what your browser already receives every time it connects here.
